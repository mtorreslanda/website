Water quality from space

Estimating turbidity and chlorophyll from Sentinel-2 where there are no labs, and testing whether it says anything about waterborne disease.

The question

Humanitarian settings rarely have water labs. Can a satellite estimate water quality there, and does that estimate help anticipate diarrhoea or cholera?

What I did

  • Reproduced the state of the art. The optical model follows a published approach (mixture density networks trained on satellite–lab matchups), rebuilt at scale on Earth Engine.
  • Validated it where it would be used: country by country with real Sentinel-2 data, not simulated spectra.
  • Found why it failed to transfer: pixels contaminated by shore and bridges. Keeping only clean pixels cut the error in every country tested (e.g. turbidity 115% → 85% in Argentina, 72% → 57% in Canada).
  • Quantified the small-water gap: only 28% of children who drink surface water have at least one hectare of permanent water within 5 km — what a 10–20 m satellite can see.

Results, including the null ones

Household survey data with GPS in nine countries showed no detectable link between satellite turbidity and child diarrhoea, ruling out effects larger than about 1.5–2 percentage points per tenfold change. Around seven African lakes, turbidity anticipates cholera inland as much as on the shore, which reads as a regional climate signal rather than a lake reservoir.

So the project reproduced and confirmed the existing method, then mapped precisely where it stops being useful. That map is the contribution.
